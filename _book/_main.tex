\chapter{An Introduction to Hypothesis Testing}\label{an-introduction-to-hypothesis-testing}

In brief, hypothesis testing is an approach to assessing the statistical evidence for a claim through the analysis of data from a random sample. The claim will typically relate to some aspect of the distribution of a population of interest, often a \emph{parameter} such as the \emph{population mean}, \(\mu\), and the foundation of hypothesis testing lies in \emph{probability}.

\subsection*{The Logic of Hypothesis Testing}\label{the-logic-of-hypothesis-testing}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{The Logic of Hypothesis Testing}

Consider the following scenarios:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A coin is tossed 50 times, and it lands showing tails 47 of those times.
    \item A cubical die is tossed 80 times, and a six is obtained only once.
    \item A random number generator gives the same number eight times in a row.
\end{itemize}

In each of these scenarios, there are two possible conclusions:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
     & \textit{The coin, die and number generator are all fair,}& \\
     & \textit{and a very unlikely event simply happened by random chance...} & \\
     && \\
     & \textbf{or} & \\
     && \\
     & \textit{The coin is biased, the die rigged, the random number generator flawed...} &
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

An \emph{objective} process is required that allows a decision to be made between these two possibilities. This process, called \textbf{hypothesis testing}, will form a substantial part of the remainder of the course.

\section{The Null Hypothesis and the Alternative Hypothesis}\label{the-null-hypothesis-and-the-alternative-hypothesis}

Most statistical studies begin with a claim, which is called the \textbf{alternative hypothesis}, or \(\text{H}_\text{1}\). Initially in this chapter, these will consist of a claim about the value of the \emph{population mean}, \(\mu\). A conservative stance in opposition to the alternative hypothesis will be used as the basis for all calculations, assuming that the claim is \emph{not true}. This is called the \textbf{null hypothesis}, or \(\text{H}_\text{0}\). These may be thought of \emph{informally} as:

\begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{llll}
        The null hypothesis & $\text{H}_\text{0}$ & \textit{``Nothing meaningful is happening.''}\\
        The alternative hypothesis & $\text{H}_\text{1}$ & \textit{``Something meaningful is happening.''}
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

It is \emph{essential} that both hypotheses are formed \emph{before} observing the data. It should also first be decided whether evidence is being sought for the true value of the population mean being \emph{less than} a specified value, \emph{greater than} a specified value, or simply \emph{different from} a specified value. When the alternative hypothesis is only interested in a \emph{single direction}, the test being performed is described as \textbf{\emph{one-tailed}}. When the alternative hypothesis is interested in a change in \emph{either direction} from the specified value, it is referred to as a \textbf{\emph{two-tailed}} test.

\begin{tcolorbox}[title=Example,colback=RoyalBlue!1!,colframe=RoyalBlue]
\textbf{Problem:} State the null and alternative hypotheses for each of the following claims.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(a)] The mean volume of liquid in espressos sold by a café is less than the 30ml stated on their menu. 
    \item[(b)] The mean mass of red squirrels in a woodland population is different to the previously measured 240g.
\end{enumerate}

\tcblower
\textbf{Solution:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \begin{multicols}{2}
    \item[(a)] $\text{H}_\text{0}:$  $\mu=\text{30}$ \quad \textnormal{  One-tailed.}\\ $\text{H}_\text{1}:$  $\mu<\text{30}$
    \item[(b)] $\text{H}_\text{0}:$  $\mu=\text{240}$ \quad \textnormal{Two-tailed.}\\ $\text{H}_\text{1}:$  $\mu\ne\text{240}$
    \end{multicols}
\end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection*{Evidence to Suggest}\label{evidence-to-suggest}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Evidence to Suggest}

The \emph{true value} of a population parameter cannot be known by studying a sample. This means that, without a census of the entire population, a statistician will never say that they have \emph{proved} or \emph{disproved} a claim, and statistical conclusions are always expressed in terms of evidence, rather than certainty. A suitable conclusion should make a judgement on whether there is \textbf{sufficient evidence to suggest} or \textbf{insufficient evidence to suggest} a particular claim (represented by \(\text{H}_\text{1}\)) is true.

\subsection*{\texorpdfstring{The Significance Level and the \textit{p}-value}{The Significance Level and the -value}}\label{the-significance-level-and-the--value}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{The Significance Level and the \textit{p}-value}

Once the null and alternative hypotheses have been stated, a conservative viewpoint is taken - it is \emph{assumed that the null hypothesis is true}. Only an observed value that is then \emph{very unlikely to have occurred by random chance} will lead to the decision to \textbf{reject the null hypothesis} in favour of the alternative hypothesis. The decision to \emph{reject or not reject} is made with respect to the \emph{null hypothesis}, whilst the \emph{evidence (to suggest)}, or lack of, relates to the \emph{alternative hypothesis}.

The probability of observing a result at least as extreme as the one obtained, assuming the null hypothesis is true and in the direction specified by the alternative hypothesis, is called the \(\mathbf{p}\)\textbf{-value}. A decision should be made in advance of collecting the data regarding the \textbf{significance level}, \(\alpha\) (\emph{``alpha''}). A significance level of 5\% (\(\alpha=\text{0.05}\)) is commonand is often used when no other significance level has been specified. It can be interpreted as the level of `unlikeliness' that it would take in order to abandon \(\text{H}_\text{0}\) in favour of \(\text{H}_\text{1}\).

When the \(p\)-value is less than \(\alpha\) this is referred to as a \emph{statistically-significant} result, and smaller \(p\)-values indicate stronger evidence against the null hypothesis.

\begin{centering}
\tcbset{width=16cm}
\begin{tcolorbox}[adjusted title=The decision rule for the $p$-value and the significance level $\alpha$:, halign title = center,colback=red!5,colframe=red!70!black, halign = center,center]
\centering
If $p\text{-value}\leqslant\alpha$ then \textbf{reject }$\mathbf{\text{H}_\text{0}}$ and conclude that\newline there is \textit{sufficient evidence to suggest} that the  alternative hypothesis is true.\\
\vspace{0.5cm}
If $p\text{-value}>\alpha$ then \textbf{do not reject }$\mathbf{\text{H}_\text{0}}$ and conclude that\newline there is \textit{insufficient evidence to suggest} that the alternative hypothesis is true.
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{centering}

Failing to reject the null hypothesis does not prove that it is true; it simply indicates that there is insufficient evidence against it. To avoid suggesting that evidence will ever be found for the null hypothesis, the phrase `accept \(\text{H}_\text{0}\)' should \textbf{not be used}.

  \centering
\begin{minipage}{.45\textwidth}

The level of significance influences the long-run risk of making a \textit{Type I Error} - incorrectly rejecting the null hypothesis when it is in fact true. Failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is in fact false is called a \textit{Type II Error}.

\end{minipage}%

When performing \emph{any} significance test the following procedure is generally performed:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt]
    \item Determine \textbf{which hypothesis test} is appropriate for the data and the focus of the study.
    \item Decide on a suitable \textbf{significance level} ($\alpha$).
    \item State the \textbf{null hypothesis} ($\text{H}_\text{0}$) and the \textbf{alternative hypothesis} ($\text{H}_\text{1}$).
    \item Calculate the \textbf{test statistic} under the assumption that \textit{the null hypothesis is true}.
    \item Determine the \textbf{critical value}, or calculate the $p$-value.
    \item Decide whether to \textbf{reject or not reject} $\text{H}_\text{0}$.
    \item Write a suitable statistical \textbf{conclusion}.
\end{itemize}

A conclusion should always be written in context, referring to the original claim.

\section{\texorpdfstring{One-Sample \textit{z}-Test for the Population Mean using a \textit{p}-value}{One-Sample -Test for the Population Mean using a -value}}\label{one-sample--test-for-the-population-mean-using-a--value}

The \emph{one-sample }\(z\)\emph{-test for the population mean} is a hypothesis test used when a sample of data is taken from a population so that a claim about the \emph{true value of the population mean}, \(\mu\), can be assessed. Since \(z\)-tests rely on assumptions about population parameters and the normal distribution, they are classed as \textbf{parametric tests}.

\begin{centering}
\tcbset{width=16cm}
\begin{tcolorbox}[adjusted title=Conditions for the one-sample $z$-test for the population mean:, halign title = center,colback=red!5,colframe=red!70!black, halign = center]
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textit{sampling distribution of the sample mean} is \textbf{normal} (or approximately normal)
    \item The \textit{population standard deviation}, $\sigma$, is \textbf{known}.
    \item The observations are \textbf{independent} (typically assured through \textit{random sampling}).
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{centering}

If one or more of these conditions is not known to be satisfied, then it is required to make an \textbf{assumption} that it \emph{is} satisfied for the test to be valid. In any statistical study assumptions that have been made should be recognised and consideration should be given as to whether they could be justified.

Note that when the sample size is greater than 20 \textbf{the CLT can be invoked}, so the sample mean is at least \emph{approximately} normally distributed regardless of the distribution of the underlying population. Also, for large sample sizes, the sample standard deviation, \(s\), may be used as a reliable estimate of the population standard deviation, \(\sigma\).

Probabilities relating to observed sample means have already been covered in Chapter 9, and the following examples will demonstrate how this leads to a calculated \(p\)-value for a hypothesis test based on a sample mean. Note below that, with a sample mean of 257 observed, and an alternative hypothesis that the true population mean is \emph{less than} 260, the \(p\)-value is the probability of observing a sample mean \emph{as extreme or more extreme} than 257. In the context of a claim that \(\mu<\text{260}\), the \(p\)-value is therefore \(\text{P}\left(\bar{X}<\text{257}\right)\).

\section{\texorpdfstring{One-Sample \textit{z}-Test for the Population Mean using a Test Statistic}{One-Sample -Test for the Population Mean using a Test Statistic}}\label{one-sample--test-for-the-population-mean-using-a-test-statistic}

Obtaining a \(p\)-value in the previous section required calculating the probability of the observed sample mean, \(\bar{x}\), by first using the \(z\)\emph{-transformation for a sample mean} to obtain what is called a \(z\)\emph{-score}. Instead of concluding a \(z\)-test by comparing a \(p\)-value to the significance level, an alternative approach is to compare the \(z\)-score, referred to as the \textbf{test statistic}, to predetermined cut-off values, called \textbf{critical values}.

\begin{centering}
\tcbset{width=10cm}
\begin{tcolorbox}[adjusted title=For a one-sample $z$-test for the population mean:, halign title = center,colback=red!5,colframe=red!70!black, halign = center]
Test statistic: $z=\dfrac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}}$
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{centering}

A \(z\)\emph{-test statistic} of 0 occurs when the sample mean of the observed data is equal to the value for \(\mu\) claimed by the null hypothesis. The further the test statistic is from 0, the more extreme the observed data is.

\emph{Critical values} can be found by first considering \emph{critical regions}. These are the most extreme regions of the sampling distribution of the test statistic; if the calculated test statistic lies within these regions, the null hypothesis is rejected. Critical values are the \emph{boundaries} of these critical regions. Below are examples of critical regions (shown shaded), and critical values (shown below the axis), for a \(z\) test statistic with a 5\% level of significance.

For a one-tailed test, the most extreme 5\% of the distribution in the tail of interest is considered.

For a two-tailed test, the 5\% least-likely observed values for the test statistic \emph{in either direction} are considered. The significance level is split between 2.5\% in the upper tail and 2.5\% in the lower tail. The symmetry of the distribution means that the values to the left of the mean are the \emph{negative} of the equivalent values on the right.

If the test statistic lies \textbf{within the critical region} then \(\mathbf{\text{H}_\text{0}}\) is \textbf{rejected}, and so there is \textit{sufficient} evidence to suggest that the alternative hypothesis is true. If the test statistic lies \textbf{outwith the critical region} then \(\mathbf{\text{H}_\text{0}}\) is \textbf{not rejected}, and so there is \textit{insufficient} evidence to suggest that the alternative hypothesis is true.

\section{\texorpdfstring{One-Sample \textit{t}-Test for the Population Mean}{One-Sample -Test for the Population Mean}}\label{one-sample--test-for-the-population-mean}

In the early 20th century William Sealy Gosset, statistician and Head Experimental Brewer at the Guinness factory in Dublin, described the distribution of the sample mean when the \textbf{population standard deviation is unknown}. This was necessary since he was working with \textbf{small sample sizes}, for which the sample standard deviation could not be used as a reliable estimator for the population standard deviation. Since Guinness did not want rival companies to know that they were employing a statistician to improve their brewing, he published his work under the pen name \emph{Student}, and the distribution he described became known as \textbf{Student's} \textbf{\emph{t}} \textbf{-distribution}, or simply \emph{the} \(t\)-\emph{distribution}.

Not knowing the population standard deviation, \(\sigma\), introduces additional uncertainty about the distribution of the sample mean, with the \textbf{sample standard deviation}, \(s\), used instead. This results in a \(t\)-distribution that resembles a normal distribution but with \emph{fatter tails}. The \(t\)-distribution takes one parameter called \textbf{degrees of freedom}, denoted using the Greek letter \(\nu\), which is the number of values that are free to vary once the sample mean has been fixed. For the one-sample \(t\)-test for population mean, the degrees of freedom can be calculated for a sample size \(n\) as \(\nu=n-\text{1}\).

As the sample size increases, and hence \(\nu\) increases, the \(t\)-distribution approaches the normal distribution. This is why the Data Booklet provides a set of critical values for use when \(\nu>\text{40}\), which match critical values for \(z\)-tests.

The one-sample \(t\)-test for population mean is a \textbf{parametric test} that should be used in place of the equivalent \(z\)-test when the population standard deviation, \(\sigma\), is not known, and the sample standard deviation, \(s\), is to be used instead.

\begin{centering}
\tcbset{width=14.5cm}
\begin{tcolorbox}[adjusted title=Conditions for the one-sample t-test for the population mean:, halign title = center,colback=red!5,colframe=red!70!black, halign = center]
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textit{underlying population} is \textbf{normally distributed}.
    \item The observations are \textbf{independent} (typically assured through \textit{random sampling}).
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{centering}

The \(t\)-test statistic is identical to that of the equivalent \(z\)-test, except the use of the sample standard deviation, \(s\), instead of the population standard deviation, \(\sigma\).

\begin{centering}
\tcbset{width=12cm}
\begin{tcolorbox}[adjusted title=For a one-sample t-test for the population mean:, halign title = center,colback=red!5,colframe=red!70!black, halign = center]
Test statistic: $t=\dfrac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}}$ \; with $\nu=n-\text{1}$ degrees of freedom
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{centering}

Critical values for the \(t\)-test can be found on \emph{page 13} of the Data Booklet, referring to the significance level, whether the test is one-tailed or two-tailed and the degrees of freedom; constructing a sketch of the distribution is often useful.

\section{\texorpdfstring{One-Sample \textit{z}-Test for the Population Proportion}{One-Sample -Test for the Population Proportion}}\label{one-sample--test-for-the-population-proportion}

If \(X\) elements from a random sample of size \(n\) from a population of interest satisfy some condition, then the \textbf{sample proportion}, \(\hat{P}\), can be calculated as:

\[\hat{P}=\frac{X}{n}\]

For example, if a survey of registered voters reveals 114 out of 250 intend to vote for the incumbent candidate, the \emph{sample proportion}, \(\hat{p}\), is \(\frac{\text{114}}{\text{250}}=\text{0.456}\). The true value of the \textbf{population proportion}, \textbf{\emph{p}}, may differ from this value. The \textbf{one-sample \(z\)-test for proportion} assesses whether an \emph{observed sample proportion} provides statistically significant evidence against a \emph{null-hypothesised population proportion}. The test statistic can be derived from the distributions of the random variables \(X\) and hence \(\hat{P}\):

\[X \sim \text{B}(n,p)\]

When \(np>\text{5}\) and \(nq>\text{5}\), \(X\) is \emph{approximately} normally distributed:

\begin{align*}
\text{E}(X) & = np\\
\text{V}(X) & = npq\\
X & \approx \mathsf{N}(np,npq)
\end{align*}

The distribution of \(\hat{P}\) can now be obtained since \(\hat{P}=\frac{X}{n}\):

\[\mathsf{E}\left(\hat{P}\right)=\text{E}\left(\frac{X}{n}\right)=\frac{\text{1}}{n}\text{E}(X)=\frac{\text{1}}{n}\times np=p\]

\[\text{V}\left(\hat{P}\right)=\text{V}\left(\frac{X}{n}\right)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^\text{2} \text{V}(X)=\frac{\text{1}}{n^\text{2}}\times npq=\frac{pq}{n}\]
Therefore:

\[\hat{P} \approx N\left(p,\frac{pq}{n}\right)\]

The \(z\)-transformation leads to the required test statistic:

\begin{centering}
\tcbset{width=10cm}
\begin{tcolorbox}[adjusted title=For a one-sample $z$-test for the population proportion:, halign title = center,colback=red!5,colframe=red!70!black, halign = center]
Test statistic: $z=\dfrac{\hat{p}-p}{\sqrt{\dfrac{pq}{n}}}$
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{centering}

Note that \(\sqrt{\dfrac{pq}{n}}\) is called the \textbf{standard error of the sample proportion}.

\begin{centering}
\tcbset{width=15cm}
\begin{tcolorbox}[adjusted title=Conditions for the one-sample z-test for the population proportion:, halign title = center,colback=red!5,colframe=red!70!black, halign = center]
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{\textbf{\emph{np}}>\text{5}}$ \textbf{ and } $\mathbf{\textbf{\emph{nq}}>\text{5}}$
    \item The observations are \textbf{independent} (typically assured through \textit{random sampling}).
\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{centering}

\pagebreak

Critical values for the \(z\)-test for population proportion are determined in the same way as for the \(z\)-test for the population mean.

\begin{tcolorbox}[title=Example,colback=RoyalBlue!1!,colframe=RoyalBlue]
\textbf{Problem:} A data storage company knows that 10\% of the hard drives they use will fail within the first year of their use. They have decided to trial the use of a cheaper model of hard drive, but are concerned that it may have a higher failure rate. During this trial, 60 of the new hard drives are randomly selected to be monitored for a year once installed. Of those 60, 11 fail.\\ 

Use a parametric hypothesis test to assess the evidence, at the 5\% level of significance, that the new drives have a higher first-year failure rate than the previous model.

\tcblower
\textbf{Solution:}
$p=\text{0.1}$ , $q=\text{0.9}$ , $n=\text{60}$ , $\hat{p}=\frac{\text{11}}{\text{60}}=\text{0.183}$\\

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{math}
\begin{array}{ll}
      \text{H}_\text{0}: & p=\text{0.1}\\
      \text{H}_\text{1}: & p>\text{0.1}
    \end{array}
\end{math}\\

\vspace{0.2cm}

Test statistic: $z=\dfrac{\text{0.183}-\text{0.1}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{\text{0.1} \times \text{0.9}}{\text{60}}}}=\text{2.14}$

Since the alternative hypothesis for this test refers to the population \emph{proportion}, conclusions should always aim to refer to a `proportion' in context (and never the \emph{mean}).

\textbf{Problem:} A candidate in a mayoral election claims that 85\% of voters intend to vote for them. Out of a random sample of 80 people eligible to vote, 59 say that they intend to vote for that candidate. \tcblower
\textbf{Solution:}
$p=\text{0.85}$ , $q=\text{0.15}$ , $n=\text{80}$ , $\hat{p}=\frac{\text{59}}{\text{80}}=\text{0.7375}$\\

$np=\text{80}\times \text{0.85}=\text{68}$ and $nq=\text{80}\times \text{0.15}=\text{12}$.

\section{Choosing the Right Hypothesis Test}\label{choosing-the-right-hypothesis-test}

Selecting the appropriate hypothesis test to use in any given context is an important skill for a statistician and warrants careful practice. The following questions contain a mix of \(z\)\textit{-tests for the population mean, }\(t\)\textit{-tests for the population mean and }\(z\)\textit{-tests for the population proportion.}

    \begin{enumerate}[resume]
        \item Perform a parametric test to assess, at the 5\% significance level, whether there is evidence to support the newspaper's claim.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}
